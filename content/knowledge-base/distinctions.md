---
id: d-explicit-vs-inferred
title: Explicit versus inferred
category: distinctions
themes: [interpretation, evidence]
related: [p-evidence-before-inference, ig-quote-and-anchor, ca-do-not-invent-facts]
---

Explicit is what the participant wrote. Inferred is what it might indicate. The interpretation
must keep the two visibly separate and never present the second in the grammar of the first.

Explicit: "I haven't taken a full week off in four years."
Inferred: "Rest appears to be something you defer rather than schedule."

The inference may be wrong — perhaps they took long weekends constantly, perhaps the work is
seasonal. Marking it as an inference costs one clause and preserves the participant's ability to
correct it. Presenting it as fact makes the whole reflection less trustworthy the moment one
such claim misses.

---
id: d-value-stated-vs-value-enacted
title: Stated values versus enacted values
category: distinctions
themes: [values, time, tension, evidence]
related: [p-respect-contradictions, v-time-as-finite, ig-surfacing-a-tension]
---

What a person says matters and what their described week shows are two different data sources,
and both are legitimate.

Neither automatically overrides the other. The cynical reading — that behaviour reveals "real"
values and words are self-deception — is too quick. A stated value that is not yet enacted may
be a genuine commitment newly formed, or a value held under conditions that make acting on it
expensive.

The useful move is to hold both and describe the distance between them without adjudicating.
"You name creative work as what makes time feel well spent, and describe a week with no time in
it for creative work" states the situation completely. The participant is better placed than the
system to say which of the two is the aspiration.

---
id: d-inherited-vs-chosen
title: Inherited versus chosen
category: distinctions
themes: [values, identity, responsibility]
related: [c-creation-of-values, v-chosen-over-inherited, t-approval-vs-authenticity]
---

An inherited value arrived without being selected — from family, culture, profession, faith,
or the expectations of people whose approval once mattered. A chosen value has been examined and
kept.

The distinction is not a hierarchy. Plenty of inherited values survive examination intact, and
the framework is not asking anyone to discard their upbringing. What it asks is whether the
examination has happened.

Markers of the inherited register: obligation phrasing, vagueness about reasons, borrowed
vocabulary, a stated goal the participant describes without any evident appetite. Markers of the
chosen: specific reasons the participant owns, willingness to name the cost, and consistency
under the pressure of the finitude question.

---
id: d-desire-vs-commitment
title: Desire versus commitment
category: distinctions
themes: [action, discipline, becoming]
related: [d-outcome-vs-action, p-toward-action, ig-priorities-and-commitments]
---

"I want to" and "I will" are different states, and the gap between them is where most of the
exercise's material lives.

A desire has no cost attached. A commitment specifies what is given up: the hour, the evening,
the other opportunity, the person disappointed. Almost every stated desire that has gone
unrealised for years is a desire that has never been converted into a commitment, and the
missing step is nearly always naming the trade.

When a participant describes something they have wanted for a long time and not done, the
generative question is not motivational. It is: what would you have to stop doing? An
interpretation that surfaces the unnamed trade is doing real work.

---
id: d-comfort-vs-safety
title: Comfort versus safety
category: distinctions
themes: [courage, fear, discipline, action]
related: [v-courage-and-voice, c-self-overcoming, t-approval-vs-authenticity]
---

Staying comfortable and staying safe feel identical from the inside and are not the same thing.

Comfort is the absence of friction now. Safety is the absence of harm over time. They frequently
point in opposite directions: the uncomfortable conversation protects the relationship, the
uncomfortable medical appointment protects the decade, the uncomfortable financial reckoning
protects the retirement.

Participants routinely describe comfort in the vocabulary of prudence — being realistic, not
rocking the boat, waiting for the right moment. Naming the distinction is often more useful than
any encouragement, because it reframes the choice accurately: not brave versus sensible, but
short-term ease versus long-term protection.

---
id: d-busy-vs-creating
title: Busy versus creating
category: distinctions
themes: [time, work, creativity, action]
related: [v-creation-and-contribution, c-drift-versus-intention, t-creation-vs-consumption]
---

Activity and creation are easily confused, especially by people who are genuinely working very
hard.

Busy consumes time and leaves little behind. Creating leaves something that exists afterwards —
a product, a body of work, a relationship deepened, a problem solved for someone. The exercise
asks which activities generate real value and which merely consume time, and the answers are
often uncomfortable for people whose weeks are entirely full.

Note that maintenance is not the same as busywork. Sustaining a family, a team or a health
routine leaves something behind and simply does not look like output. Do not mistake care work
for wasted motion; the distinction is about what remains, not about visible productivity.

---
id: d-outcome-vs-action
title: Outcomes versus actions
category: distinctions
themes: [action, discipline, wealth, health]
related: [d-desire-vs-commitment, ig-priorities-and-commitments, v-responsibility]
---

An outcome is a result the participant does not fully control. An action is a behaviour they do.

"Earn more", "get healthy", "be a better father", "grow the business" are outcomes. They depend
on markets, bodies, other people and time. Committing to an outcome produces guilt when it does
not arrive, and offers nothing to do on a Tuesday.

"Send three proposals a week", "walk after dinner", "call on Sunday", "one hour on the thing
before email" are actions. They are wholly within the participant's control, and they can be
kept or broken observably.

Every commitment the system proposes must be an action, and it must derive from something the
participant identified themselves. This is the single most common failure in generated advice —
restating the participant's outcome back to them as though it were a plan.

---
id: d-reaction-vs-choice
title: Reaction versus choice
category: distinctions
themes: [responsibility, action, identity, becoming]
related: [v-responsibility, c-drift-versus-intention, c-old-and-emerging-self]
---

The exercise offers the participant a set of contrasts for describing their own transformation:
the old version waited, accepted, reacted; the new version acts, speaks, chooses.

Reaction is responding to what arrives. Choice is deciding in advance what one is for and
letting that govern. Most lives contain a great deal of the first, and this is not a character
flaw — reaction is what a full life under pressure produces.

The distinction is useful in interpretation because participants frequently describe wanting to
"be less reactive" without identifying what they would choose instead. Reaction abhors a vacuum;
it is only displaced by something specific. Where a participant has named what that something
is, quote it back — they have supplied the substance themselves.
