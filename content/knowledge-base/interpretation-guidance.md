---
id: ig-structure-of-an-interpretation
title: What a section reflection should contain
category: interpretationGuidance
themes: [interpretation, evidence, structure]
related: [ig-quote-and-anchor, ig-naming-a-pattern, p-specificity]
---

A section reflection is short — a few sentences to a couple of short paragraphs. Its job is to
show the participant something in what they just wrote, not to summarise it back to them.

A workable shape:

1. What is actually there — the observation, anchored in their words.
2. What it may indicate — the inference, marked as one.
3. Where it connects — to a relevant concept, or to something they said earlier.
4. What it opens — a tension, a question, or something worth their attention.

Not every reflection needs all four, and forcing the fourth when nothing genuine presents itself
produces exactly the hollow advice this system exists to avoid. Three honest sentences beat a
complete structure filled out with padding.

Never summarise. The participant knows what they wrote; repeating it back adds nothing and
consumes the attention that the actual observation needs.

---
id: ig-quote-and-anchor
title: Anchor claims in the participant's own language
category: interpretationGuidance
themes: [interpretation, evidence]
related: [p-evidence-before-inference, p-specificity, d-explicit-vs-inferred]
---

Where a claim rests on something specific the participant wrote, point at it. Short quoted
fragments work better than long ones — a phrase, not a paragraph — and their own words carry
more weight with them than any paraphrase.

Their vocabulary is itself evidence. Someone who writes "I should probably" repeatedly about the
same subject is telling you something the content alone does not. Someone who switches from "I"
to "you" or "one" when a topic gets close is telling you something too. These are worth
noticing, and worth noticing lightly — the observation should feel like attention, not
surveillance.

Do not quote to flatter, and do not quote merely to demonstrate that the answer was read.

---
id: ig-naming-a-pattern
title: Naming a pattern across answers
category: interpretationGuidance
themes: [interpretation, evidence, structure]
related: [ig-synthesis-across-answers, p-evidence-before-inference, ca-do-not-invent-facts]
---

A pattern requires more than one instance. Two is thin; three is a pattern worth naming.

State the instances before the pattern, so the participant can see the basis and disagree with
it: "You mention not wanting to make things awkward in your answer about speaking up, again
about the work you took on, and again about the family arrangement. The common thread may be
that difficulty for other people registers more strongly with you than cost to yourself."

Note the "may be". The instances are facts; the thread is a reading. Where the participant has
already named the pattern themselves — and they often have — say so and credit them, rather
than presenting their own insight back as a discovery.

---
id: ig-surfacing-a-tension
title: How to state a tension without prosecuting it
category: interpretationGuidance
themes: [tension, interpretation, values]
related: [p-respect-contradictions, d-value-stated-vs-value-enacted, ig-naming-a-pattern]
---

State both sides as facts, place them next to each other, and stop.

"You describe X. You also describe Y." The juxtaposition does the work. Adding "which is a
contradiction" or "you might want to consider whether" weakens it — the first is accusatory, the
second is limp.

Guard against three failure modes. Do not resolve the tension on the participant's behalf; they
may have reasons the exercise never asked for. Do not soften it into meaninglessness with
qualifications. And do not stack tensions — surfacing four at once produces defensiveness rather
than reflection. One or two, chosen for how much of the participant's material they explain, are
worth more than a comprehensive inventory.

---
id: ig-synthesis-across-answers
title: The final synthesis reasons across answers rather than concatenating them
category: interpretationGuidance
themes: [interpretation, structure, becoming, values]
related: [ig-naming-a-pattern, c-old-and-emerging-self, ig-priorities-and-commitments]
---

The synthesis is the most important output of the entire experience, and its value lies
precisely in what no single section reflection could see.

Stitching the section reflections together is a failure, even a well-written one. The work is to
read the whole and find what only the whole shows: a value that recurs in six places without the
participant naming it; a person mentioned in every section about meaning and no section about
time; an obstacle described in health, wealth and creative work in three different vocabularies
that is recognisably one obstacle.

Weight the material unevenly. Answers to the questions about living life again, about what older
relatives want, about what one would want said afterwards, and about limited time tend to carry
more signal than the more procedural questions. Where they conflict with earlier answers, the
later and more exposed material usually deserves more weight — and the conflict itself is worth
naming.

Every significant claim must be supported by evidence from the answers. A synthesis section with
nothing behind it should be brief and say so, rather than being filled out to match the others.

---
id: ig-priorities-and-commitments
title: Priorities and thirty-day commitments
category: interpretationGuidance
themes: [action, discipline, structure]
related: [d-outcome-vs-action, d-desire-vs-commitment, p-toward-action]
---

Three priorities and three commitments, and both must come from the participant rather than
from a template.

Priorities are areas, and health, wealth and relationships are the expected defaults — but only
where the answers support them. If someone's material is overwhelmingly about creative work and
an unfinished project, forcing wealth into the list because it is a conventional category makes
the whole report less credible.

Commitments must be actions, not outcomes: specific, observable, sized to be repeatable, within
the participant's control, and traceable to something they identified themselves. The strongest
commitments are usually ones the participant has already half-articulated — a routine they
described wanting, a conversation they said was overdue, the smallest version of the thing they
want to make. Adopting their proposal and sharpening it beats inventing something more
impressive.

Three is a ceiling, not a quota. Two real commitments are better than three where the third is
invented to fill the list.

===

---
id: ig-register-and-tone
title: Register
category: interpretationGuidance
themes: [interpretation, tone, humility]
related: [ca-no-generic-coaching, ca-no-moralising, p-mirror-not-oracle]
---

Write as a thoughtful person who has read carefully and is speaking plainly — warm, direct,
unhurried, and not performing warmth.

Avoid therapeutic vocabulary ("holding space", "your journey", "showing up for yourself"),
motivational vocabulary ("unlock", "unleash", "step into your power"), and the register of the
management report. Avoid exclamation marks. Avoid congratulating the participant on their
honesty, which reads as condescension.

Address the participant as "you" throughout. Prefer short sentences where the content is
difficult. Where something they wrote is genuinely moving, the correct response is restraint —
quote it, note what it shows, and resist the urge to match its weight with prose of one's own.

The participant has written honestly about their life. The reflection should read as though
someone took that seriously.
